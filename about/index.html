About

Science shaped by curiosity, movement & connection.

I am a cell and molecular biologist whose scientific journey has moved across disciplines, countries and research environments — while remaining centred on a recurring interest in how changes within cells become meaningful biological consequences.

Scientific Biography

Following the biological question.

I have always been most interested in science when one biological question naturally leads to another.

My research path has taken me from cellular stress and redox biology to extracellular vesicles, endothelial ageing, cellular senescence, molecular profiling and quantitative functional cell biology.

Although these areas can appear distinct, I see a continuous thread between them: understanding what changes inside a cell, how those changes influence communication with other cells, and how that communication ultimately produces a biological effect.

Current Status

Based in Paris, France. Open to new research positions, scientific collaborations and translational biomedical R&D opportunities.

Foundations

From India to Ireland.

My scientific journey began in India and developed internationally through postgraduate education and research in Europe.

Undergraduate training in life sciences at the University of Delhi gave me a broad biological foundation before I moved to Ireland for postgraduate study in neuropharmacology at the University of Galway.

During this period, I became particularly interested in how cellular stress, mitochondrial dysfunction and molecular regulation contribute to biological dysfunction and disease.

Subsequent research in redox biology deepened that interest by focusing on how proteins respond to changes in the intracellular environment and how reversible molecular modifications can regulate cellular function.

A Translational Turn

Discovering extracellular vesicles.

Working in an industry-linked research environment changed the scale at which I thought about cellular biology.

My early exposure to therapeutic extracellular-vesicle research introduced the idea that biological communication systems could potentially be understood not only mechanistically, but also developed towards translational applications.

This was an important shift in perspective. The question was no longer only what happens inside a cell, but also what cells release, how biological material moves between cells, and whether these processes can eventually be understood or manipulated for useful biomedical purposes.

France & Germany

Ageing, senescence & communication.

My doctoral research brought cellular ageing and intercellular communication together.

Through the Marie Skłodowska-Curie TiCardio network and an international cotutelle between Aix-Marseille Université and Johannes Gutenberg University Mainz, I investigated endothelial ageing, cellular senescence and extracellular-vesicle biology.

Working across international research environments also broadened how I approached scientific questions. Experimental cell biology, molecular profiling and collaborative research became complementary parts of the same investigation rather than independent technical activities.

This period shaped the scientific perspective I still carry today: changes in cellular state matter not only because of what happens inside the affected cell, but because those changes may alter the information exchanged throughout a biological system.

Paris

From communication to measurable function.

Postdoctoral research in Paris shifted my attention increasingly towards quantitative functional biology.

During my fixed-term appointment in the Lavieu Lab at Université Paris Cité, I worked with quantitative mammalian cell-based systems for evaluating functional cargo delivery.

My work focused on optimisation and standardisation of quantitative reporter-based functional assays, reproducible experimental workflows, experimental controls, quantitative analysis and structured troubleshooting.

This experience strengthened an important distinction in how I think about delivery biology: detecting cellular uptake is not necessarily equivalent to demonstrating functional intracellular delivery. The biological question ultimately requires a measurable functional readout.

Scientific Identity

Mechanism, measurement and meaning.

I am most comfortable working at the intersection of mechanistic biology and quantitative experimentation.

I value experiments that answer clearly defined biological questions, controls that make interpretation defensible and workflows that can be reproduced rather than merely demonstrated once.

This perspective has developed through work across academic, collaborative and translational research settings. It is also why I am increasingly interested in technologies that allow complex biological questions to be measured more rigorously and translated into useful decisions.

Future Direction

From biological understanding to future technologies.

Looking forward, I want to contribute to research and development that translates biological understanding into technologies with practical relevance for human health.

I am particularly interested in R&D at the interface of cell and molecular biology, quantitative experimentation and technology development.

In the longer term, I would like to contribute to future biomedical technologies across areas such as therapeutics, diagnostics, precision medicine and personalised healthcare.

This does not mean limiting my future work to any single disease area or experimental platform. I am interested in scientific environments where strong mechanistic biology can be translated into better ways to understand disease, identify meaningful biological signals, evaluate interventions and ultimately improve healthcare.

Emerging technologies — including advanced cell-based systems, molecular profiling, quantitative imaging, computational approaches and AI-assisted research tools — are especially interesting to me where they strengthen biological discovery and translational decision-making.

Science as a Community

Research also happens through conversation.

Science has always been more meaningful to me when it is shared, discussed and challenged by people with different perspectives.

Alongside laboratory research, I have taken part in scientific outreach, researcher representation, mentoring and community activities across the different environments in which I have worked.

In Paris, this developed further through co-founding Gene & Tonic — an informal science communication initiative designed to bring researchers and wider communities together around scientific ideas and conversation.

Outside the Laboratory

Curiosity does not stop at science.

Living and working internationally has shaped me beyond research as much as it has shaped my scientific career.

I enjoy travelling, discovering new places and cultures, playing guitar and spending time outside the laboratory exploring the environments around me.

Moving between India, Ireland, France and Germany has also made international environments feel natural to me. I enjoy meeting people with different backgrounds and perspectives — something that carries directly into how I approach scientific collaboration.
